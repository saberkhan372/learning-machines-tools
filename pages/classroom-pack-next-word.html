Fluent Is Not Necessarily True: A Next-Word Investigation

An adaptable, ready-to-run classroom activity. Students predict how a sentence continues, compare guesses, and confront the gap between a likely-sounding answer and a true one. Runs in a browser, on paper, or as pure discussion — no AI account required.

Optional browser tool ↗ No-AI pathway ↗
01

Audience & adaptation range

Designed for grades 6–12, undergraduates, and adult / professional development. No computer-science background is needed for the teacher or students. It can fit English language arts, computer science, social studies, media and information literacy, and library instruction. For younger learners, use one short, concrete prompt and keep the focus on prediction versus verification. For advanced groups, add the data and evaluation layer in the 60-minute route. These grade bands are adaptation guidance, not the result of a formal classroom validation study.

02

Three timed versions

15 min
Two quick prediction rounds + a verified answer + one discussion question. Good as a warm-up or exit activity.
30 min
Two rounds (open prediction, then the truth-gap round), a short tally, the reveal, and guided discussion. The core version.
60 min
Adds the data/defaults layer ("who decides what's likely?"), a student-authored sentence, and the bounded-claim write-up (§14–15).

15-minute route

  1. 0–2: frame prediction as different from fact-checking.
  2. 2–6: run Round 1 and tally the spread.
  3. 6–10: run Round 2, reveal the verified answer, and use the branch note in §06.
  4. 10–13: discuss one verification question from §11.
  5. 13–15: complete one bounded claim from §15.

30-minute route · core

Use the exact six-step protocol in §05: 2 + 7 + 9 + 4 + 6 + 2 minutes.

60-minute route

  1. 0–5: frame the activity and make silent predictions.
  2. 5–15: run Round 1, tally, and compare the spread.
  3. 15–27: run Round 2, verify the answer, and discuss why a tally is not a source.
  4. 27–37: name the documented mechanism and the teaching analogy's limits (§08–10).
  5. 37–47: groups write a new sentence stem and predict how changing its context may change the distribution.
  6. 47–55: compare next tests and the evidence each would require.
  7. 55–60: complete and share the Field Note (§14–15).
03

Learning objective

Students can explain, in their own words, that an autoregressive language model uses learned parameters and the current context to assign probabilities to possible continuations, and that a fluent or confident continuation is not necessarily true. They can name one piece of evidence they would need before treating an AI-produced statement as a fact.

04

Materials & setup

  • A board, chart paper, or shared doc to tally guesses — or hands / sticky notes.
  • The two sentence prompts in §06 (project them or read aloud).
  • Optional: the Next-Token Prediction Game (browser, no login). Its probability display is an authored, illustrative teaching approximation, not output from a live model.
  • The printable Field Note (§14), one per student, for the 30/60-minute versions.

No AI account, no student log-ins, and no generation are required for any version.

05

Step-by-step facilitation protocol (30-minute core)

  1. Frame (2 min). "For a few minutes, we'll perform one simplified prediction task: choose a plausible next word. This human activity is an analogy, not a model simulation."
  2. Round 1 — open prediction (7 min). Show the open prompt (§06). Everyone writes one word. Tally the room's answers on the board. You will often get several plausible words rather than one. Ask: why more than one? what made you pick yours? Name the documented connection carefully: autoregressive language models assign probabilities to possible continuations from their learned parameters and the current context. The room tally only makes the idea of a distribution visible.
  3. Round 2 — the truth gap (9 min). Show the second prompt (§06). Tally again, then verify the answer with a source. Use the branch note whether the room favors Sydney, Canberra, or another answer. The tally can show what was common in this room; it cannot establish which answer is true.
  4. Name the idea (4 min). "Prediction can produce fluent text, but prediction alone does not guarantee factual accuracy." Unsupported or false generated content is often called a hallucination or confabulation. This activity illustrates why probability and verification are different; it does not reproduce every cause of those failures.
  5. Discuss (6 min). Use §11.
  6. Close (2 min). Each student completes the bounded-claim frame (§15) about what they saw.
06

Exact prediction prompts

Round 1 · open prediction (several answers may be plausible)

The cat sat on the ________.

Round 2 · compare a room tally with a verified fact

The capital of Australia is ________.

Round 2 branch note

Verify the answer before class: Canberra. If Sydney or another city leads, ask why a familiar answer felt plausible. If Canberra leads, ask whether a correct majority makes the tally a reliable fact-checking method. Either result supports the bounded point: popularity and fluency are not evidence of truth. Do not present this as a prediction of how a particular AI product will answer.

07

Worked example · hypothetical, facilitator-created

Imagine a class of 24 produces: Sydney (16), Melbourne (5), Canberra (2), unsure (1). This is invented demonstration data, not a result from a Learning Machines participant group. Sydney is the room's most common answer; a source check shows that the capital is Canberra. The evidence supports: "Sydney was the most frequent guess in this hypothetical tally, but frequency did not establish truth." It does not support a claim about what a particular language model would output or why.

08

What this models

  • The distinction between prediction and verification: a continuation can be plausible without being source-checked.
  • A distribution: several possible next words can receive different weights rather than there being one required continuation.
  • The core warning: fluent, confident, or popular does not necessarily mean true. Next-token prediction helps explain why factuality is not automatic, but it is not a complete account of model confabulation.
09

What it does not model (say this out loud)

The activity is a teaching analogy

The room is not a neural network. The board tally is not a model's internal distribution. Predicting by hand shows the idea of likely-next-word; it does not reproduce any real model's architecture.

A real model is not a corpus lookup

A language model assigns probabilities from learned patterns across huge amounts of text — not by counting a phrase in a database at the moment you ask. Training frequency influences outputs, but the output is not a direct count or a fact-check.

10

What evidence would verify a mechanism claim

If a student claims "the model said Sydney because Sydney appears more often in its training data," treat that as a hypothesis, not a finding. A responsible test would record the product and model/version if known, date, exact prompt and context, relevant settings, and complete output; repeat the prompt under controlled variations; and consult the model card, system documentation, or architecture paper. A claim about training frequency would additionally require relevant dataset evidence, which may not be available. Even then, observed outputs do not by themselves reveal one internal cause. This activity establishes only the conceptual distinction between probability and verification.

11

Discussion questions

  • Why did Round 1 produce several answers instead of one? What does that tell you about "prediction"?
  • In Round 2, which answer was most common? What made it feel plausible, and did the tally establish that it was true?
  • If a chatbot answered a homework question in fluent, confident language, what would you check before trusting it — and how?
  • When is "the most likely answer" good enough, and when is it dangerous? (Autocomplete a text vs. a medical dose vs. a citation.)
  • Whose text trained the patterns? Whose sentences count as "likely," and whose might be missing?
12

No-AI version (paper / discussion only)

Run the entire activity with no device: read the two prompts aloud, collect guesses by show of hands or sticky notes, tally on the board, verify the factual answer from a prepared source, and discuss. The activity itself requires no account, network submission, or generated output. This is the recommended route for restricted networks and for groups that do not want to use generative AI. See the full No-AI pathway for more.

13

Access, privacy & age considerations

  • Privacy: the paper route does not require students to submit names or work to an online service. The optional browser tool needs no login and does not intentionally transmit typed guesses; avoid pasting student names. GitHub Pages, Google Fonts, and aggregate GoatCounter analytics process page requests under their own terms and settings.
  • Age: the pack is written for grade 6 and up, but that range has not been formally validated. Adapt vocabulary, examples, pacing, and independent browsing to your learners and local requirements.
  • Access: the printable is legible in grayscale and does not depend on color. Read prompts aloud for print/vision needs; the activity works fully by voice and paper.

Authoritative sources & further reading

  • On this site: the Next-Token Prediction Game and the Session 1 · Text overview.
  • Fact source for Round 2: Australian Government Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, “About Australia” (lists Canberra as the capital city). Check the source again before teaching rather than relying on this pack alone.
  • Transformer architecture: Vaswani et al. (2017), “Attention Is All You Need”. This is a primary technical paper, not an age-adjusted classroom explanation.
  • Autoregressive language modeling: Brown et al. (2020), “Language Models are Few-Shot Learners”. It documents GPT-3's autoregressive design and important limitations.
  • Confabulation and verification risk: National Institute of Standards and Technology (2024), AI Risk Management Framework: Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1).

These sources support the documented mechanism and risk framing. They do not validate the room activity as a literal model simulation or establish learning outcomes for a particular grade band.
