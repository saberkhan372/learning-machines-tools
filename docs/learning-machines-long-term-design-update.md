# Learning Machines — Long-Term Design Update

> **Status:** Long-term direction after the Summer 2026 pilot. This is not an immediate rebuild checklist. It records what should remain distinctive, what should become clearer, and how a future version can evolve without turning into a conventional institutional curriculum.
>
> **Last updated:** July 19, 2026

## Executive direction

The next version of Learning Machines should become more distinctly a **field studio**, not a more polished conventional curriculum.

The central difference is:

> Other programs teach people to use AI or learn facts about AI. Learning Machines teaches people how to make defensible claims about AI.

Everything—name, sessions, activities, site language, guests, fieldwork, recaps, and tools—should reinforce that distinction.

Learning Machines should remain:

- informal rather than credential-driven;
- hybrid across physical activity, browser tools, discussion, drawing, critique, and asynchronous work;
- interdisciplinary across education, art, computing, media studies, design, and cultural inquiry;
- accessible without paid accounts, prior coding experience, or required generative-AI use;
- technically honest about the limits of its simulations and metaphors;
- built around participant observations rather than passive delivery;
- open to enthusiasm, ambivalence, criticism, and refusal.

The next version does not need a new pedagogical identity. It needs to make its existing identity easier to recognize, enter, and carry elsewhere.

---

## 1. Name and identity

### Keep the name

**Learning Machines** is memorable, open-ended, and slightly strange. It should not be replaced with an institutional title such as “AI Literacy for Educators.”

Add a clarifying subtitle:

> **Learning Machines**<br>
> *A field studio for how generative systems work, fail, and shape culture.*

An alternate event subtitle is:

> Text · Images · Motion · Human Judgment

Use “camp” for the live cohort and “field studio” for the broader project:

- **Learning Machines Summer Camp** — the live cohort experience;
- **Learning Machines Field Studio** — the ongoing public project;
- **Learning Machines Field Manual** — the website and its reusable materials.

### Core tagline

> Perform the mechanism. Test the output. Bound the claim. Decide what humans do next.

### Four-line argument

Carry this argument across the homepage, slides, field notes, and closing synthesis:

- Fluent ≠ necessarily true
- Plausible ≠ necessarily neutral
- Smooth ≠ evidence
- Possible ≠ appropriate

The first three describe the relationship between convincing outputs and warranted conclusions. The fourth returns responsibility to people.

---

## 2. Program promise

Avoid broad promises such as “understand AI” or “use AI responsibly.” They do not describe the distinctive work participants actually do.

Use this promise instead:

> Participants investigate generative systems through prediction, performance, comparison, and critique. They leave with a better mental model, one claim they can support with evidence, and one way to bring the investigation into their own practice.

The recurring method is the real curriculum:

1. **Predict.**
2. **Perform or run the system.**
3. **Change one thing.**
4. **Compare evidence.**
5. **Make a bounded claim.**
6. **Name what the activity cannot prove.**
7. **Decide what a human should do next.**

This loop should be more prominent than any individual tool or model architecture.

---

## 3. Audience and stance

Name the primary audience more specifically:

> Educators, artists, creative technologists, organizers, skeptics, and curious people who want more than prompt tips but do not need a computer-science course.

Explicitly welcome different relationships to the technology:

- I use these systems.
- I am curious but cautious.
- I am actively critical.
- I teach about them.
- I want to make something.
- I do not want to use generative AI directly.

Do not promise one experience for “all ages.” The live workshop is strongest for adults, older students, educators, and interdisciplinary groups. Classroom adaptations can serve younger learners through separate activity cards and run notes.

Refusal remains a full participation route, not a consolation pathway.

---

## 4. Format: an informal hybrid field studio

### What hybrid means

Hybrid should describe more than a combination of Zoom and a website. It should refer to the learning modes:

- human performance;
- drawing and making;
- browser-based simulations;
- frozen examples;
- technical explanation;
- cultural interpretation;
- live conversation;
- asynchronous fieldwork;
- classroom translation;
- critique and refusal.

Use a small cohort—roughly 12–24 live participants. That is large enough to reveal group patterns and small enough to treat participant observations as material.

### Standard 120-minute rhythm

Each session should have a recognizable structure:

| Time | Mode |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Re-entry and one retrieval question |
| 10–35 | Embodied centerpiece |
| 35–50 | Literal mechanism bridge |
| 50–70 | Instrumented A/B investigation |
| 70–80 | Break |
| 80–100 | Human layer: labor, defaults, consent, power |
| 100–115 | Guest response or participant studio |
| 115–120 | One bounded claim and one remaining question |

This is a rhythm, not a script. The embodied activity can expand when it produces genuine inquiry, but the evidence claim and analogy limit should never disappear at the end.

### Guest role

Guests should respond to what the room has produced rather than deliver detached closing lectures.

Send each guest:

- the prior Field Reports;
- three unresolved participant questions;
- two artifacts from that day;
- the session’s analogy limits;
- the exact time boundary and recording boundary.

Invite artists, researchers, teachers, organizers, critics, and builders who can complicate the session’s model rather than merely endorse it.

---

## 5. Program arc

### Prelude · Choose Your Stance

**Format:** 10–15 minutes asynchronous<br>
**Purpose:** establish access, consent, and starting position without creating a prerequisite course.

Participants:

- choose their participation stance;
- select a Use, Observe, Teach, Build, or No-AI route;
- review the consent boundaries;
- bring one text, image, or video whose authority they are unsure how to judge;
- write a one-line starting claim about AI that they may later revise.

The starting claim becomes a quiet pre/post artifact rather than a formal test.

---

### Session 1 · The Machine Continues

**Plain-language subtitle:** Text, prediction, context, and preference<br>
**Argument:** Fluent ≠ necessarily true

#### Guiding question

> What has to happen between a prompt and the next token—and which parts are mistaken for understanding?

#### Centerpiece: The Room as a Probability Distribution

Everyone completes the same sentence stem. Tally the room before revealing a toy model distribution.

Ask:

- Which answer was common?
- Which answer was plausible but rare?
- Does popularity establish truth?
- What does this activity model?
- What does it leave out?

#### Literal mechanism bridge

Keep three ideas visible:

- text becomes tokens;
- context changes possible continuations;
- decoding selects among possibilities.

Attention, temperature, and preference tuning should appear as different operations, not one large “AI thinking” cloud.

#### Investigation

Use one contrast sequence rather than touring every text tool:

1. Count the Next Token;
2. Tokenizer + Temperature Visualizer;
3. ELIZA rule inspection versus a pre-generated LLM response.

#### Human layer: Whose Preference?

Participants rank two responses, then expose that alignment criteria come from people and institutions.

Include warmth, sycophancy, and therapy framing here:

> Sounding attentive is an output pattern, not evidence of care.

#### Field note

> The output did ___, supported by ___. This suggests ___, but it cannot establish ___.

---

### Session 2 · The Prompt Leaves Blanks

**Plain-language subtitle:** Images, conditioning, data, and defaults<br>
**Argument:** Plausible ≠ necessarily neutral

#### Guiding question

> When a prompt leaves something unspecified, what fills the blank?

#### Centerpiece: Human Diffusion Canvas, two-pass version

Run two deliberately different simulations:

- **Run A:** everyone receives the full prompt and refines the drawing through several passes;
- **Run B:** phrases are revealed sequentially.

The comparison teaches the analogy’s boundary:

- Run A is closer to full-prompt conditioning.
- Run B makes commitment and cultural convergence visible.
- Neither recreates an actual diffusion model.

#### Literal mechanism bridge

Keep four components visible:

- prompt representation;
- noisy image or latent field;
- iterative prediction and removal of noise;
- repeated sampling with no unique correct image.

#### Investigation: the Default Test

Use an underspecified role:

- a doctor;
- a teacher;
- a scientist;
- a family;
- or a role chosen from the participant’s own discipline.

Participants document:

- what appeared;
- what they did not request;
- what changed after one revision;
- what remained stubborn;
- what remains only a hypothesis about training data.

#### Human layer

Map:

- who created or appeared in source material;
- who labeled or captioned it;
- whose conventions became “normal”;
- who is absent;
- what cannot be inferred from one generated image.

#### Field note: Default Map

> Requested / supplied by the system / culturally familiar / possible source / cannot conclude

---

### Session 3 · The Frame Has a Past

**Plain-language subtitle:** Motion, coherence, source evidence, and provenance<br>
**Argument:** Smooth ≠ evidence

#### Guiding question

> What has to remain related across time—and why does visual smoothness not authenticate a clip?

#### Centerpiece: Coherence Animator

Develop Coherence Animator as the defining next-version activity.

Participants draw a short sequence under two reference conditions:

- previous frame only;
- fixed anchor plus previous frame.

Then they:

- play both sequences;
- follow one chosen feature;
- mark where it drifts;
- compare the two reference conditions;
- explain what the comparison can and cannot establish about real video models.

The browser should display the path the participant marks. It should never imply that it automatically discovered motion.

#### Literal mechanism bridge

Distinguish:

- tracking correspondence in existing frames;
- generating a new frame;
- preserving identity and spatial relationships;
- producing motion that looks plausible;
- satisfying physical constraints.

#### Investigation: evidence, not glitch spotting

Use the Video Failure Gallery, but give every clip a hypothetical real-world claim:

- security evidence;
- eyewitness footage;
- accident documentation;
- footage from a private home.

The central question becomes:

> What would you need before treating this clip as evidence?

#### Human layer

Connect:

- source and chain of custody;
- corroboration;
- labor and likeness;
- consent;
- content credentials;
- the difference between provenance and truth.

Keep the key line:

> A provenance record can document a chain. It cannot retroactively supply consent.

#### Field note: Video Evidence Report

> Exact observation / possible explanation / source evidence required / bounded conclusion

---

### Optional Studio · The Human Decides

**Plain-language subtitle:** What we made, found, taught, changed, or refused<br>
**Argument:** Possible ≠ appropriate

Do not make this a polished showcase. Participants bring one thing they:

- made;
- found;
- taught;
- questioned;
- revised;
- or deliberately refused.

Use three rotating clinics.

#### Evidence clinic

Is the claim supported and appropriately bounded?

#### Classroom clinic

What would make this usable with actual learners?

#### Consequence clinic

Who is affected, represented, excluded, documented, or asked to consent?

End by revisiting the participant’s opening claim from the Prelude.

## 6. Activity design rules

Every core activity should have six visible parts:

1. **Prediction**
2. **Action**
3. **Comparison**
4. **Literal mechanism**
5. **Analogy limit**
6. **Human decision**

### Teaching Cartoon Cards

Add a standard block beside every major analogy and simulation:

> **What this models**<br>
> The relationship or constraint the activity helps make visible.
>
> **What it does not model**<br>
> The ways the real system differs.
>
> **What would verify the claim**<br>
> Documentation, source evidence, or experiment needed.

Call these **Teaching Cartoon Cards**. The phrase gives the project permission to be accessible while preventing a classroom analogy from quietly becoming the technical explanation.

### General activity constraints

- One centerpiece per session.
- No more than two supporting browser instruments in the main live route.
- A no-AI route must reach the same conceptual objective.
- Authored simulations must identify themselves as simulations.
- Frozen examples must identify their source and intended evidentiary use.
- The debrief must include the limit of the analogy.
- Every participant should leave with a small artifact.
- Technical accuracy takes priority over a punchy but categorical phrase.

---

## 7. Participant-facing language

Keep stable URLs, but gradually change the visible language:

| Current label | Next-version label |
|---|---|
| Tools | Lab instruments |
| Worksheets | Field notes |
| Assignments | Between-session fieldwork |
| Recaps | Field Reports |
| Facilitation docs | Run notes |
| Prompt packs | Frozen evidence packs |
| Tool catalog | Instrument cabinet |

Use the themed term alongside plain language at first—for example, “Field notes · printable worksheets”—so the identity does not reduce clarity.

Avoid framing that anthropomorphizes systems or promises mastery. Prefer:

- system;
- model;
- output;
- representation;
- pattern;
- evidence;
- inference;
- human decision;
- missing information;
- teaching cartoon.

Use “AI literacy” as a secondary description for institutions and searchability, not as the project’s primary voice.

---

## 8. Between-session fieldwork

Reduce the number of primary assignment routes to three.

### Test

Run one controlled comparison and document what changed.

### Translate

Adapt one activity for a classroom, community, artistic, or disciplinary setting.

### Critique or refuse

Analyze a frozen example, consent problem, policy, dataset, or reason not to use a system.

“Build” can remain an advanced extension rather than a fourth competing default.

Every route produces the same small artifact:

- what I examined;
- what I changed or compared;
- what evidence I have;
- what I think it suggests;
- what it cannot prove;
- what I would do next.

Keep the phrase **one thing you can point to**. It communicates the right scale and favors evidence over polish.

---

## 9. Field Reports

Recaps should become part of the method rather than documentation after the fact.

Each Field Report should contain:

1. What the room investigated
2. What participants predicted
3. What the activity revealed
4. Two bounded claims
5. Corrections to the live explanation
6. Where the metaphor broke
7. Participant artifacts and quotations with consent
8. Unresolved questions for the next session
9. Recording and fieldwork links

Publicly showing corrections is a strength. It demonstrates the epistemic behavior the workshop teaches.

Field Reports should distinguish clearly among:

- a participant observation;
- a facilitator interpretation;
- a technical mechanism claim;
- a source-verified factual statement;
- an unresolved question.

---

## 10. Site architecture

### Homepage

The homepage should become smaller and more directed:

1. What Learning Machines is
2. The four-line argument
3. Start with one five-minute investigation
4. The four sessions
5. The repeated method
6. No-AI participation
7. Recent Field Reports
8. Full instrument cabinet

Do not lead with the complete tool inventory.

### Session pages

Every session page should use the same five doors:

- **Perform**
- **Inspect**
- **Question**
- **Make**
- **Teach**

These are more specific to the project than generic difficulty levels while still supporting different participation styles.

### Instrument pages

Every instrument should display:

- simulation / real model / frozen example / role-play / field note;
- estimated time;
- individual / pair / room mode;
- beginner / bridge / technical level;
- what it makes visible;
- what it cannot establish;
- its No-AI equivalent;
- its associated session and field note;
- relevant technical sources.

### Instrument cabinet

Keep experimental and specialized tools, but place them behind an **Instrument cabinet** rather than presenting every tool as an equal starting point.

The essential path should contain one primary activity and no more than two supporting instruments per session.

### Source and version visibility

Mechanism explanations should include:

- the architecture or family they describe;
- whether the explanation is general or model-specific;
- the date last reviewed;
- primary technical sources;
- a correction history when wording materially changes.

---

## 11. Participation and engagement design

Do not interpret chat volume as the room’s understanding.

To distribute interpretation more evenly:

- require silent prediction before open chat;
- collect one Field Note from every participant;
- use pairs before plenary discussion;
- rotate observer, maker, and skeptic roles;
- invite two artifacts selected during the studio period;
- allow spoken, written, drawn, coded, and refusal-based responses;
- ask for one observation before asking for opinions;
- let guests respond to quiet participants’ artifacts as often as highly visible comments.

### Role structure

Small groups can rotate among:

- **Maker** — performs the activity or creates the artifact;
- **Observer** — records exact changes and evidence;
- **Skeptic** — names what the activity cannot establish;
- **Translator** — connects the result to another discipline or classroom.

These roles make interdisciplinary expertise productive instead of allowing technical confidence to dominate.

---

## 12. Informal assessment

Do not add quizzes, badges, or institutional grading to the core experience.

Use repeated evidence instead.

At the beginning and end of the series, give participants the same overconfident claim:

> “The model understood the prompt and created the correct result.”

Ask them to revise it.

Growth becomes visible when the revised version distinguishes:

- observation from inference;
- mechanism from metaphor;
- output quality from truth;
- possibility from appropriateness;
- documentation from consent.

Also track, without grading:

- one Field Note per session;
- one analogy limit named;
- one claim revised after new evidence;
- one classroom or disciplinary transfer;
- one question the participant now knows how to investigate.

### Recurring transfer prompt

Use the same four questions across modalities:

1. What entered the system?
2. What transformation or selection occurred?
3. What evidence supports that description?
4. What human choices or missing source information matter?

This provides continuity without pretending that text, image, and video systems all use identical architectures.

---

## 13. What not to do

- Do not become a generic “AI tools for teachers” workshop.
- Do not chase every new model release.
- Do not add more tools until the essential path is obvious.
- Do not make ethics a final discussion slide.
- Do not make direct AI use the default marker of participation.
- Do not claim to teach all of machine learning.
- Do not replace creative ambiguity with institutional vocabulary.
- Do not let guests become detached keynotes.
- Do not hide corrections or failed activities.
- Do not turn participants into passive consumers of polished explainers.
- Do not mistake a memorable metaphor for a literal architecture description.
- Do not make completion, polish, or public sharing conditions of full participation.

---

## 14. Development roadmap

### Phase 1 · Identity and pruning

- Adopt the field-studio subtitle and four-line argument.
- Select one centerpiece and two supporting instruments per session.
- Classify every existing instrument by type, level, time, and evidentiary boundary.
- Move nonessential tools into the Instrument cabinet.
- Identify outdated or architecture-specific claims that require review.

**Deliverable:** a smaller essential path without removing the deeper archive.

### Phase 2 · Activity revision

- Turn Human Diffusion Canvas into a two-pass comparison.
- Continue Coherence Animator around drawing, animation, participant-marked tracking, provenance, and bounded claims.
- Standardize Teaching Cartoon Cards.
- Create one shared Field Note format across modalities.
- Add a clear No-AI equivalent to every core activity.

**Deliverable:** three centerpieces that share one inquiry method while preserving modality differences.

### Phase 3 · Site revision

- Simplify the homepage.
- Reframe assignments as between-session fieldwork.
- Reframe recaps as Field Reports.
- Add Perform / Inspect / Question / Make / Teach structure to session pages.
- Make simulation versus real-model status impossible to miss.
- Add time, mode, level, evidence boundary, and source metadata to instrument pages.

**Deliverable:** a participant-facing site whose structure communicates the pedagogy before someone opens a tool.

### Phase 4 · Small interdisciplinary pilot

Recruit 8–12 people, including:

- two educators;
- two artists;
- two technical participants;
- two critical or refusing participants;
- several mixed-background participants.

Test only:

- whether the metaphor maps;
- whether its limit is understood;
- whether everyone produces a bounded claim;
- whether the activity works without direct AI use;
- whether the site makes the starting route obvious.

**Deliverable:** revised activities and language based on observed use, not only facilitator judgment.

### Phase 5 · Full next cohort

Run the complete three-session arc plus optional Studio.

Publish:

- Field Reports;
- corrections;
- consented artifacts;
- revised instruments;
- anonymized aggregate participation findings;
- a short account of what changed because participants used the materials.

**Deliverable:** a documented second cycle that can support future partnerships without turning the project into a standardized course.

---

## 15. Measures of success

The next version is successful if:

- participants can make more bounded claims by the end than at the beginning;
- every core metaphor includes a visible limit;
- every participant can contribute without a paid account or direct generation;
- the essential path is understandable without browsing the complete catalog;
- fieldwork produces small, inspectable artifacts rather than unfinished ambitious projects;
- guests respond to participant inquiry rather than sit outside the curriculum;
- critical questions emerge from mechanisms rather than being appended as an ethics module;
- participants transfer at least one activity or question into another discipline;
- corrections remain visible as part of the project record;
- the workshop retains its informal, unfinished, communal quality.

---

## Final design principle

> Make Learning Machines less like a resource library with a workshop attached, and more like a living interdisciplinary investigation whose instruments, field notes, guests, corrections, and participants all contribute to the same argument.

The project’s long-term value is not that it contains the largest number of AI explainers. Its value is that it gives people a repeatable way to investigate convincing machine output without surrendering judgment to it.

## Appendix A · Field positioning (July 2026 landscape scan)

> **Status:** Evidence supporting the *Executive direction*. A web scan of the AI-education landscape (July 2026) tested whether the central claim—"other programs teach people to use AI or learn facts about AI; Learning Machines teaches people how to make defensible claims about AI"—is genuinely differentiated or merely aspirational.
>
> **Finding:** The differentiation is real, but it rests on a *combination* and one outcome reframe, not on any single feature. Every individual ingredient exists somewhere in the field; the braid does not.

### The six clusters Learning Machines sits between

Each established tradition optimizes for one thing. Learning Machines diverges from each on the axis that matters.

| Cluster | Representative work | Optimizes for | Where Learning Machines diverges |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mainstream AI-literacy PD for educators | UNESCO AI Competency Framework; university educator guides | *Use*—prompting, output-as-draft, productivity, adoption | Refuses the "AI tools for teachers" frame (§13); the outcome is a defensible claim, not competence |
| Structured K–12 AI curricula | AI4K12 "5 Big Ideas"; Code.org AI Lab; Elements of AI | *Building* and standards coverage | Anti-curriculum, informal, adult/interdisciplinary; centers doubt over construction |
| Interactive explainers | Distill.pub (dormant); 3Blue1Brown; Transformer Explainer | *Visualizing mechanism* | Passive, single-mode, mechanism-only; lets the visualization stand in for the architecture—the exact failure Teaching Cartoon Cards (§6) correct |
| Art + AI programs | Gray Area Creative Code Intensive; MIT Media Lab Creative AI; SFPC; RISD | *Making work with* AI as a creative partner | Epistemically driven, not production-driven; free and remote; refusal is a full route (§3), not a footnote |
| Media literacy / provenance | C2PA & Content Credentials; synthetic-media literacy | *Authenticating* media, often via detection heuristics | Rejects glitch-spotting as authentication; integrates provenance into the generation mechanism (Session 3) |
| Critical AI studies | Kate Crawford / AI Now; Data & Society | *Theory*—AI as socio-technical system, labor, power | Operationalizes the theory into a 120-minute activity a practitioner can run |

A researched pedagogy cuts across all six—embodied/unplugged AI (AI Unplugged, Georgia Tech; MIT Media Lab; The Tech Interactive). Learning Machines already works in this tradition but has not yet connected to its published evidence base.

### What the scan confirms is genuinely distinctive

No single item below is unique. The scan found no program combining them.

> **The outcome is epistemic, not instrumental.** Others end at "can use AI," "understands how AI works," or "can make art with AI." Learning Machines ends at "can make a bounded, evidence-backed claim and decide what a human should do next." The scan found no other program framing the deliverable as *judgment* rather than competence or mastery. This is the moat.

- **Refusal as a full participation route.** Critical AI studies discusses refusal; Learning Machines operationalizes it. The scan found essentially no comparable program doing so.
- **One argument across three modalities** (fluent ≠ true / plausible ≠ neutral / smooth ≠ evidence / possible ≠ appropriate). Most programs are single-modality; the four-line spine is a thesis, not a syllabus.
- **Instruments honest about being cartoons.** The Teaching Cartoon Card corrects a flaw shared by both the explainer tradition and mainstream PD. It is the closest thing the project has to ownable intellectual property.
- **Accessibility and rigor held together.** The field usually forces a trade—free-but-shallow or rigorous-but-gated. Free, no-account, no-coding, informal, and intellectually serious at once is rare.
- **Visible fallibility—the one a funded competitor structurally cannot copy.** Publishing corrections, "where the metaphor broke," and participant observation as material is a trust signal an institutional PD vendor or an art school cannot afford to imitate.

### Where the field is moving—and how to deepen against it

Three currents from the scan, each with a corresponding move. These extend the roadmap (§14); they do not replace it.

**Current 1 — "critical evaluation of outputs" is becoming the field’s named competency.** Learning Machines is ahead of this, but the academic vocabulary is forming now, and mainstream PD will borrow the word "critical" while keeping the instrumental outcome.

> **Deepen:** Name and publish the method as a citable framework. The seven-step loop (§2) and Teaching Cartoon Cards (§6) are a real contribution currently buried inside a workshop. A short, reusable, nameable "field method" plants the flag before the language dilutes. *Highest-leverage move.*

**Current 2 — embodied/unplugged AI is now researched pedagogy with efficacy claims.** Learning Machines does this in the wild without connecting to the evidence base.

> **Deepen:** Turn Field Reports (§9) and aggregate participation data into a small evidence base. The project already collects engagement and retention data most programs never gather; aligning with or citing embodied-AI research borrows legitimacy and separates the project from the vibes end of the workshop world.

**Current 3 — provenance is becoming infrastructure, not a topic.** C2PA / Content Credentials adoption is accelerating as after-the-fact detection loses the arms race.

> **Deepen:** Make Session 3 touch real provenance—inspecting an actual Content Credential as a field instrument, not only the concept. This stays technically honest and current without chasing model releases (§13).

Two further moves tied to the moat rather than the currents:

- **Own "bounded claims" as the signature everywhere.** It is the clearest wedge and no one else stands on it. Lead with it on the homepage (§10), in program copy, and in every Field Report.
- **Formalize the artist + technologist + critic bridge.** Guests already triangulate this. Art+AI and critical-AI-studies rarely speak to each other; being the bridge is a position neither cluster can occupy alone. Make it explicit in guest recruitment (§4).

### Cautions

- **The nearest neighbors are genuinely close.** MIT Media Lab’s plugged/unplugged Creative AI and the academic critical-AI-literacy strand overlap on individual axes. The defense is never a single feature—it is the combination. Do not let any one competitor pull the project onto its axis.
- **"Field studio" language is illegible to institutions searching for the project.** Keep "AI literacy" as a secondary, searchable term (§7). The poetry is for participants; the plain term is for discovery.
